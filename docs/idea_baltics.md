# The idea, at Baltic scale

## In one line

**Baltic sport can't see its own fans. We give each federation one fan identity across every club and platform, then unify them into a single Baltic fan graph, so for the first time the whole market can be sold to sponsors as one product instead of scattered national fragments.**

## The problem

Across Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia, a single real fan exists as several separate users. Someone who attends a national-team match, then a top-flight club game, then a lower-league fixture is registered across different ticketing platforms, or simply walks through a cash gate and is never recorded at all. The data sits with the ticketing vendors, not with the clubs and not with the federations. Clubs receive partial reports at best. Cash-gate and free-entry clubs capture nothing.

The consequence is that no federation has a unified view. None can say how many unique fans its football actually has, how often they attend, how they move between clubs and the national team, or what their demographics are. Everything below the aggregate is a guess.

That guess is what kills sponsor money. A sponsor cannot value an audience it cannot see. Baltic sport can only show fragmented per-platform numbers, so it cannot offer the whole market as one product. Business money into sport stays low precisely because the value can't be proven.

## Why the Baltics, not just one country

The ticketing chaos is the same in all three markets, and it is run by the same company. Bilietai.lt (Lithuania), Bilešu Serviss (Latvia), and Piletilevi (Estonia) are all part of PLG, the largest ticket distributor in the region, on shared infrastructure. The fragmentation we solve is identical across the three, caused by the same vendors. That means one integration battle opens three markets, not one.

Each country has the same structure: small market, fragmented ticketing, federations with no unified fan data, sponsors who can't see value. What works for one federation works for the others with almost no change to the model.

And the endgame is bigger than three separate national databases. Owning the fan identity layer across all three countries means offering sponsors a Baltic-wide audience, not just a national one. A single Nordic or Baltic brand can reach all three markets through one product. The whole is worth materially more than the sum of the parts.

## The solution

A single sign-on identity for football, then sport, in each country. One fan, one ID, recognised across every club, ticketing platform, app, and loyalty programme. The fan logs in once instead of managing four accounts. Each club sees only its own fans. Each federation sees its whole national graph: unique fans, frequency, movement between clubs and the national team, growth trends. Above that, the three national graphs combine into one Baltic fan graph.

The federations own the data, not the vendors. Ticketing platforms become processors that feed a central customer data platform (CDP) via API, under GDPR, while ownership sits with the federations and the clubs.

## Why this works, and why it's the right idea

This is not a concept. It is a proven model. Denmark built exactly this with LigaLogin: 200,000+ registered users, running across 25+ football platforms, where the league owns the data. One mid-sized club generated around 2,000 new sign-ups in a single evening before a cup fixture. Denmark started from the same fragmented chaos the Baltics have now.

The model answers every objection that sinks simpler ideas:

- **Fans adopt it** because one account beats four. Denmark proved the adoption curve.
- **Poor clubs don't have to pay.** Each federation drives adoption by tying its support to using the system, exactly as the Danish league did. Small clubs get a professional tool for free.
- **There's no segment problem.** Big clubs and small clubs join the same system. The big clubs plug in; the small clubs, who have no CRM and no marketing staff, get one automatically.
- **The moat is the unified fan graph.** Once it exists nationally and then Baltic-wide, and the federations own it, it becomes the standard. A copycat with one club, or even one country, has nothing comparable.

## The expansion arc

- **Proof:** a pilot in one country (the national team plus one or two top-flight clubs with more resources), to show the system works, reduce vendor and club resistance, and build the first success cases.
- **National rollout:** from the pilot to the full top flight, then the lower leagues, then all of that country's football.
- **Baltic scale:** replicate the proven playbook in the second and third countries, then combine the national graphs into one Baltic fan graph sold to sponsors as a single cross-market product.

## What the federations get

For the first time, each federation can answer "how many unique fans does our football have," and can sell its market to national sponsors as one product rather than fragments from individual clubs. Combined, the three federations can offer something no single Baltic country can: one audience across three markets. That is the direct mechanism for bringing new private capital into Baltic sport: make the audience visible, and the money that couldn't be justified before starts to flow.

Football is the beachhead. The same identity layer extends across sport more broadly, which is the level the value ultimately lives at.

## The honest part

This is the LigaLogin model brought to markets that don't have it yet. That is a strength, not a weakness: a proven system, absent here, with clear owners (the national federations) who benefit directly. The hard part is not the technology. It is federation leadership and negotiating data ownership away from the ticketing vendors. Denmark solved both by making adoption the condition of league support and by writing data ownership into vendor contracts. The Baltics can follow the same path, one country at a time, then join them together.
