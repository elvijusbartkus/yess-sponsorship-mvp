# Sport Sponsorship Marketplace — Master Document

*Estonian Olympic Committee (EOK) x YESS Hackathon, Tallinn, August 2026*

*Working name: TBD. This document is the full thinking behind the idea: the problem, the solution, the marketplace model, monetization, the go-to-market, the competitive landscape, the risks, the demo scope, and the pitch. It is the proof-of-depth behind a short stage pitch, not the stage pitch itself.*

---

## 0. One-line version

**A marketplace that uses AI to match sport (clubs and athletes) with money (sponsors), and prices every match as a tax-optimized financial instrument — so private capital finally flows to the parts of Estonian and Baltic sport that today are invisible to sponsors.**

Every match we surface answers two questions at once: *who should I back?* and *what does this actually cost me after tax?* In Lithuania a €1,000 sponsorship writes €2,000 off taxable profit. In Estonia companies can give tax-free up to 3% of wages or 10% of prior-year profit, and almost nobody uses it. That capacity is sitting unclaimed, and no platform in the region surfaces it at the point of decision.

We take a commission only when a deal closes: 2% on deals above €10,000, 10% on deals below, plus performance-based upside. Nobody pays to join. We earn when we create value.

## 1. The problem

### 1.1 The headline

Estonia is a country of 1.36 million people that punches far above its weight in sport. Its athletes win hundreds of medals at international level. Surveys show that a majority of Estonians say it matters to them that Estonia wins medals. And yet only around a quarter of Estonians describe themselves as fans, and private business money into sport is the smallest of all the funding lines.

The Olympic Committee's own brief names the gap directly: how do we bring more private capital into sport? How can an athlete's value be made clearer to a sponsor? How can sport attract many smaller contributions instead of depending on a handful of large sponsors? How do the right partners find one another?

Those questions all point at the same underlying failure. Sport in Estonia and the wider Baltics cannot efficiently connect the people who need money to the people who have it. The supply of sponsorable attention exists. The demand from businesses exists. The market that should connect them does not.

### 1.2 Why the money does not flow today

There are four separate failures stacked on top of each other.

**First, discovery is broken.** A local business that wants to sponsor sport has no idea who to talk to. Which club, which athlete, which team reaches the audience they care about? There is no directory, no search, no matching. Deals happen through personal connections, luck, and whoever happens to know whom. A regional brand that would happily put a few thousand euros behind a local team never finds that team, and the team never finds the brand.

**Second, value is unprovable.** Even when a club and a sponsor do find each other, the club cannot prove what it is worth. It says "we get a few hundred people at a game" and the sponsor has no way to verify that, no way to know who those people are, and no way to compare it to any other sponsorship option. A sponsorship decision that should be a simple audience-and-price calculation becomes a leap of faith. Most businesses do not take the leap.

**Third, the market is fragmented and small.** Estonia is a small market. The Baltics together are still small. Sponsorship is dominated by a few big deals with a few big clubs, because those are the only relationships with enough visibility to be worth a large brand's time. Everyone below the top tier, the vast majority of clubs and athletes, is left out entirely. There is no mechanism for many small businesses to make many small contributions, which is exactly the model the Olympic Committee says it wants.

**Fourth, the money costs more than it has to.** Every Baltic state offers companies a tax incentive to put money into sport, and almost nobody uses it — because the incentive lives in tax law, not in the sponsorship conversation. In Lithuania a company sponsoring a club with official support-recipient status deducts twice the amount from taxable profit: €1,000 given writes €2,000 off the taxable base. In Estonia companies can give tax-free up to 3% of paid wages or 10% of prior-year profit, and that allowance goes overwhelmingly unclaimed. In Latvia relief exists but is narrower and status-gated.

The result is that sponsorship is evaluated as pure cost when a large part of it is, legally, subsidised. A CFO who would refuse a €5,000 "donation to a handball club" would approve the same €5,000 if someone showed them the after-tax number. Nobody shows them. There is no product anywhere in the region that surfaces the tax position at the moment the sponsorship decision is made. That is the fourth failure, and it is the one nobody else is solving.

### 1.3 Who is hurt

- **Small and mid-tier clubs** get crowds but no sponsors, because they cannot be found and cannot prove value.
- **Individual athletes**, especially outside the top few names, have real audiences and stories but no way to package that into something a sponsor can buy.
- **Local and regional businesses** want to support sport and reach local audiences but have no efficient way to find and evaluate opportunities.
- **The federations and the Olympic Committee** watch private capital stay on the sidelines while the ecosystem stays dependent on public money.
- **The national treasuries' own incentives** go unclaimed: policy designed to route private money into sport sits idle because it never reaches the person making the decision.

### 1.4 The core insight

The thing sport is missing is not more attention. It is a market. Attention exists all across the pyramid. What does not exist is the infrastructure that lets that attention be discovered, valued, and bought.

And the second half of the insight: sponsorship is not only an audience decision, it is a **financial** one. The Baltic states already decided, in law, to subsidise money flowing into sport. That subsidy is invisible at the point of purchase. A marketplace that prices every match after tax does not just help sponsors find sport — it makes the same sponsorship budget go up to twice as far. Build that market, surface that number, and the private capital the ecosystem needs starts to move.

## 2. The solution

### 2.1 What it is

A multi-sided marketplace for sport sponsorship. On one side: clubs and athletes with audiences to offer. On the other side: sponsors and businesses with money to spend. In the middle: AI matching that connects the right supply to the right demand based on audience, budget, region, and goals.

Think of it as the layer that lets a sponsor find, evaluate, and back a club or athlete the way any modern marketplace lets a buyer find, evaluate, and buy from a seller. Discovery, matching, evaluation, and transaction, all in one place, all built for sport.

### 2.2 The three sides

- **Supply (sport):** clubs and athletes create profiles describing their audience, reach, results, region, and story. This is what a sponsor is buying access to.
- **Demand (money):** sponsors and businesses describe their budget, their target audience, their region, and their marketing goals. This is what they want to achieve.
- **The match:** AI reads both sides and surfaces the strongest fits, with a clear reason for each match, so both parties understand why they were connected.

Channels like the Olympic Committee and TV sit around the edges as reach amplifiers: the committee gives the platform credibility and access, and broadcast exposure is part of what makes a sponsorship valuable.

### 2.3 How a deal happens

1. A sponsor comes in and completes a short quiz funnel: budget range, audience they want to reach, region, and goals.
2. Clubs and athletes have already built profiles with their own audience and reach data.
3. The AI matches the sponsor to the best-fitting clubs and athletes, explains why each is a fit, and prices each match after tax.
4. The sponsor reviews the matches, sees both the audience value and the after-tax cost of each, and connects with the ones they want.
5. A deal closes. The platform takes its commission.

### 2.4 Why a marketplace and not a tool

A single tool (say, a value calculator for one club) solves one narrow problem for one party. A marketplace solves the whole discovery-and-matching failure for everyone at once, and it compounds: every new sponsor makes the platform more valuable to clubs, and every new club makes it more valuable to sponsors. That network effect is the difference between a feature and a business.

## 3. The AI matching layer

### 3.1 What it does

The matching engine is the core of the product. It takes the structured inputs from both sides and produces ranked matches with explanations.

For a sponsor, the inputs that matter are: budget, target demographic, geographic focus, and campaign goal (brand awareness, local presence, youth engagement, national reach, and so on).

For a club or athlete, the inputs that matter are: audience size, audience demographics, geographic base, reach across channels, results and profile, and the kind of sponsor activation they can offer.

The AI matches on fit across these dimensions and, crucially, explains the match. Not just "here are five clubs" but "this club is a strong match because it reaches your target demographic in your target region at a scale that fits your budget." The explanation is what makes the match trustworthy and actionable.

### 3.1a The tax layer on top of the match

Fit is only half the recommendation. Every match also carries its tax position, computed from the sponsor's country and budget and the club's legal status:

- A Lithuanian club holding official support-recipient status: *"Your €1,000 writes €2,000 off taxable profit — roughly €300 back at 15% corporate income tax."*
- An Estonian club under the donation allowance: *"Fully tax-free under Estonia's allowance — capacity almost no company uses."*
- A club without qualifying status: *"Deductible as marketing spend — priced as pure audience value."*

This is not a disclosure footnote. It is a ranking input and a headline on every match card. Two clubs with identical audiences are not equally good buys if one of them is worth double to the sponsor's tax base, and the platform is the only place that tells them so.

### 3.1b Why this is a wedge and not a feature

A sponsorship marketplace is a copyable idea; Sponsoo in Germany already proved the model works. Tax-optimized matching is much harder to copy into, because it requires per-jurisdiction rules, recipient-status data on the supply side, and a marketplace positioned in exactly those jurisdictions. It is also the thing that gets a sponsor who was never going to sponsor to say yes — the argument shifts from "support sport" to "this is the most tax-efficient marketing euro you will spend this year."

### 3.2 Why explanations matter

A match with no reason is a guess. A match with a clear, data-grounded reason is a recommendation a sponsor can act on and justify internally. The explanation layer is what turns the AI from a black box into a broker the sponsor trusts.

### 3.3 The data problem, stated honestly

The matching is only as good as the data it runs on. And here is the honest weakness: at the start, club and athlete data is self-reported. A club says it reaches five thousand people; the sponsor has no independent way to verify that. Self-reported numbers can be inflated, and a marketplace that runs on unverifiable numbers erodes sponsor trust over time.

This is the single most important thing to solve for the platform to be credible, and it is addressed in the next section.

## 4. The trust layer (verified audience data)

### 4.1 The gap

Self-reported audience data is the fuel for matching, but it is also the platform's biggest credibility risk. Sponsors will not keep paying for matches built on numbers they cannot trust.

### 4.2 The answer

The trust layer is verified audience data captured at the source: a lightweight check-in system at events. Fans check in at a game (scan, quick verification, small incentive), which produces real, verified attendance and fan data rather than a club's own estimate. That verified data flows into the club's profile and becomes the number the AI matches on and the number the sponsor sees.

This is where the original check-in concept finds its correct home. It is not a separate product and not the thing sold to clubs. It is the integrity layer underneath the marketplace: the mechanism that turns self-reported audience claims into verified, sponsor-grade evidence.

### 4.3 Why this is a roadmap item, not a day-one build

For an early version and for a hackathon demo, profiles can start with self-reported and publicly available data, with verified data introduced as the trust upgrade over time. The platform works as a marketplace from day one; the verification layer makes it progressively more credible and more valuable to sponsors as it rolls out. Naming it as the roadmap turns the platform's weakest point (data trust) into a clear strength (a plan to solve it).

### 4.4 What verification unlocks

Once audience data is verified rather than claimed:
- Sponsors can trust the match and pay with confidence.
- Clubs can charge what they are actually worth, because the value is proven.
- The platform can price and rank matches on real numbers, making the AI matching materially better.
- The verified dataset itself becomes a moat: a growing, trusted record of sport audiences that no competitor starting from scratch can replicate.

## 5. Monetization

### 5.1 The model

The platform charges a commission on closed deals. Nobody pays to join, to list, or to browse. The platform earns only when it creates value by producing a deal.

**Tiered commission, split at €10,000 of deal value:**
- **2% on deals above €10,000.** Big sponsorships have big absolute value, so a thin percentage still produces meaningful revenue, and a low rate keeps the platform attractive for high-value deals that could otherwise be negotiated privately.
- **10% on deals at or below €10,000.** Small deals need a higher rate to be worth processing, and the absolute amounts are low enough that the rate is still easy for a sponsor to accept.

The €10,000 line is a starting point, not a law of nature; validating it is explicitly one of the pilot's jobs (§13).

**Commission and the tax benefit.** The commission is charged on top of the sponsorship, as a platform service fee, and is itself an ordinary deductible business expense for the sponsor. The sponsorship amount that reaches the club is what carries the enhanced relief. So a €1,000 Lithuanian deal costs the sponsor €1,100, of which €2,000 comes off the taxable base and €100 is deducted as a normal expense. The tax benefit is many times the commission — which is precisely why the commission is easy to charge.

**Plus performance-based upside.** Beyond the base commission, the platform can earn on performance: deals that deliver measurable results (reach, engagement, activation outcomes) can carry a performance component. This aligns the platform's incentives directly with the sponsor's outcomes.

### 5.2 Why commission-on-close is the right model

- **It removes all friction to joining.** Poor clubs and cautious sponsors both join for free, which solves the cold-start problem on both sides.
- **It aligns incentives.** The platform only wins when both sides win. That is an easy story to tell and an easy thing for a sponsor to say yes to.
- **It scales with value.** As deals get bigger and more frequent, revenue grows without needing to add huge numbers of new users.
- **It sidesteps the "clubs are too poor to pay" problem entirely.** Clubs never pay upfront. They only ever share a slice of money they would not have had without the platform.

### 5.3 Who pays

The commission is charged against the deal, effectively coming out of the sponsor's spend (the money side). The club or athlete receives their sponsorship; the platform's cut is a fraction of the deal value. The party with money pays, the party seeking money does not pay upfront, and the payment only happens when real value has been created.

### 5.4 Secondary revenue (later)

Once the marketplace has liquidity, additional revenue lines become possible: premium sponsor accounts with better analytics and priority matching, subscription tiers for high-volume sponsors, and paid access to aggregated (anonymized) audience insights. These are deliberately secondary. The core model is commission on closed deals, and the platform should not try to monetize secondary lines before it has liquidity.

## 6. Cold start and liquidity

### 6.1 The hardest problem in any marketplace

An empty marketplace is worthless. Sponsors will not come if there are no clubs and athletes worth backing; clubs and athletes will not come if there are no sponsors. This chicken-and-egg problem is what kills most marketplaces. It has to be solved deliberately.

### 6.2 The onboarding mechanics

- **Sponsors enter through a quiz funnel.** A short, guided set of questions (budget, target audience, region, goals) that both onboards them and immediately produces value: the moment they finish, they see matched opportunities. The funnel is low-friction and gives an instant payoff, which is what gets a sponsor to complete it.
- **Clubs and athletes fill their own profiles.** Self-service profile creation with audience, reach, results, and region. Free, fast, and the incentive is obvious: exposure to sponsors they could never reach otherwise.

### 6.3 Sequencing liquidity

The standard marketplace playbook applies:
1. **Build the supply side first.** Clubs and athletes are motivated because they are desperate for money and have nothing to lose by listing. Seed the platform with supply.
2. **Bring demand once supply is worth browsing.** Sponsors join when there is enough quality supply that the platform is worth their time.
3. **Do not monetize until there is liquidity.** Charge nothing until deals are actually happening. Revenue comes from matches, and matches require both sides populated.

### 6.4 The Olympic Committee as a liquidity accelerant

This is where the committee matters. An endorsement or partnership with the Olympic Committee gives the platform instant credibility with both sides, access to clubs and athletes across the ecosystem, and a channel to national sponsors. It is the single fastest way to solve cold-start, because it brings both supply and demand to the table at once through a trusted intermediary. This is why the committee sits at the center of the whiteboard: not as the buyer, but as the accelerant.

## 7. Market and scale

### 7.1 Start in one market

Estonia is the entry point, driven by the Olympic Committee context. The platform proves the model in one country: real clubs and athletes on the supply side, real local and national sponsors on the demand side, real matched deals.

### 7.2 Expand across the Baltics

The same failure exists identically in Latvia and Lithuania: fragmented sport, no discovery, unprovable value, sponsorship concentrated at the top. The same platform extends to all three markets with almost no change to the model. Three small markets combined are a meaningfully larger one.

### 7.3 The cross-market prize

The endgame is not three separate national marketplaces. It is one Baltic sponsorship marketplace where a brand can back sport across all three countries through a single platform. A regional or Nordic brand that wants Baltic-wide presence can find and fund sport across Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania in one place. That cross-market reach is something no single-country solution and no offline broker can offer, and it is the scale story that makes the platform more than a small local tool.

### 7.4 Beyond football, beyond the Baltics

The model is not football-specific. Any sport with clubs, athletes, audiences, and potential sponsors fits the same marketplace. Football is the natural beachhead because it has the most clubs and the clearest audience, but the platform generalizes across sports and, eventually, across the wider region.

## 8. Competitive landscape

### 8.1 Who else is nearby

- **Sponsoo (Germany) is the closest competitor and the most important one to name.** It is a working two-sided sponsorship marketplace: clubs and athletes list for free, sponsors search and filter, and Sponsoo takes a commission when a deal closes. That is the same core model described in this document, and its existence is the strongest available evidence that the model works commercially rather than only on paper. Sponsoo is not in the Baltics, is built for a large single-language market, and does not price matches after tax. We should name it on stage before anyone else does: *"the model is proven in Germany; nobody has built it here, and nobody anywhere has built the tax layer."*
- **Blocksport** is an enterprise fan-engagement and data platform for large clubs and leagues (big clubs, apps with tens of thousands of downloads, major partners). It serves the top of the pyramid and would not build for small Baltic clubs. It is not a sponsorship marketplace; it is a fan-data platform for big clubs.
- **Fanbaseclub** is a UK, ticketing-first club platform with fan data and membership features. It is built around clubs that already sell tickets, and it is a per-club tool, not a two-sided sponsorship marketplace.
- **Ticketing platforms** (Piletilevi, Piletitasku, Bilietai, and the wider PLG group) own buyer data and offer organizers some analytics, but they are ticketing systems, not marketplaces that match sponsors to sport.
- **Traditional sponsorship** happens offline through agencies, personal networks, and direct deals. Slow, relationship-dependent, and only works at the top where deals are big enough to justify the effort.

### 8.2 Why the space is still open

Two different gaps, and we sit in both.

**Geographically:** the one player doing the same thing (Sponsoo) is not here. Blocksport serves the top of the pyramid. The ticketing players serve transactions, not matching. Offline sponsorship serves whoever has connections. The vast majority of Baltic clubs, athletes, and local sponsors are served by nobody. That underserved majority is the market.

**Functionally:** nobody, including Sponsoo, prices a sponsorship match after tax. Every existing player treats sponsorship as an audience transaction. We treat it as an audience transaction with a financial instrument attached — and in the Baltics specifically, that instrument is unusually strong (Lithuania's 200% deduction is close to the most generous sport-sponsorship relief in Europe) and unusually unused. The market with the best tax incentive to solve this is the market where nobody has.

That combination — a proven model, absent from a region whose tax law makes it work better than anywhere else — is the opening.

### 8.3 Why competitors existing is good

A completely empty competitive landscape usually means there is no money in the space. Sponsoo's existence is the single most useful fact in this section: it de-risks the model. We do not have to argue that sponsors will use a marketplace — that is settled. We only have to argue that the Baltics deserve one and that tax-optimized matching makes ours materially better. Both are much easier arguments than the one Sponsoo had to make from scratch.

### 8.4 The moat

The defensibility is not the matching algorithm, which is copyable. The moat is three layers:

1. **The two-sided network.** Once both sides are populated, a competitor starting from zero has nothing.
2. **The verified audience dataset** built over time through the trust layer (§4).
3. **The tax-status registry.** Knowing which club in which country holds which qualifying status, kept current across three jurisdictions, is unglamorous, slow to assemble, and exactly the kind of asset a fast follower cannot buy. It is also what makes the matching engine's tax ranking possible at all.

A marketplace with liquidity, proprietary verified audience data, and a maintained cross-border tax-status registry is very hard to displace.

## 9. Risks and honest weaknesses

### 9.1 Cold start

The biggest risk. If both sides do not populate, there is nothing to match. Mitigation: seed supply first, use the Olympic Committee to bring both sides at once, do not monetize before liquidity.

### 9.2 Data trust

Self-reported audience data undermines credibility. Mitigation: the verified check-in trust layer, rolled out over time, turns claims into evidence.

### 9.3 Small market

The Baltics are small, which caps the ceiling of any single-country model. Mitigation: Baltic-wide from the strategy's core, plus the cross-market sponsorship product that only a regional platform can offer, plus expansion across sports.

### 9.4 Willingness to pay

The Olympic Committee's own research shows that stated willingness to make voluntary contributions to sport is low (a median theoretical contribution near zero in one scenario). This is a warning against any fan-donation or crowdfunding model. The platform deliberately does not rely on fans paying. It relies on sponsors paying for audience access, which is a proven commercial behavior, not a charitable one.

### 9.5 Tax accuracy and liability

If the platform states a tax benefit, it has to be right. Reliefs are capped, conditional on the recipient's registered status, and change with legislation. Mitigation: the platform presents the tax position as an indication with the conditions named, keeps the status registry as verified data rather than a claim, and does not act as a tax adviser. Getting one number wrong in public would cost more credibility than the feature earns — which is why the tax-status registry is treated as core infrastructure (§8.4), not as copy on a card.

### 9.6 Deal leakage

Once a sponsor and a club are matched, they could take the deal offline to avoid the commission. Mitigation: keep enough value inside the platform (matching, evaluation, verified data, reporting, ongoing matches) that staying is worth the commission, and structure the relationship so the platform remains the system of record for the deal.

### 9.7 Dependence on the Olympic Committee

The committee is a powerful accelerant but not a guaranteed partner. Mitigation: the platform works without an exclusive committee deal (sponsors and clubs can join directly), and there are three federations and committees across the Baltics, not one, reducing single-point-of-failure risk.

## 10. Fit with the Olympic Committee brief

The brief asked how Estonia can use its sports resources more intelligently to create greater sporting, social, and economic value, and specifically how to bring more private capital into sport. This platform answers that directly.

- **More private capital into sport:** the platform's entire purpose is to move sponsorship money from businesses into clubs and athletes that cannot access it today.
- **An athlete's value made clearer to a sponsor:** the profiles and, eventually, verified data make value explicit and comparable.
- **Many smaller contributions instead of a few large sponsors:** by making discovery and matching efficient, the platform lets many small local businesses back many small clubs and athletes, exactly the distributed-funding model the brief asks for.
- **The right partners finding one another:** that is literally what the AI matching does.
- **One meaningful problem, not all of sport:** the brief asks teams to identify one meaningful problem rather than solving everything. This platform picks one: the broken market between sport and sponsors. It solves that one thing deeply.

## 11. The MVP and demo scope

### 11.1 What to build for the hackathon

Do not build the whole marketplace. Build the single most persuasive slice: the sponsor-side match flow.

1. A sponsor completes the quiz funnel (country, budget, audience, region, goals).
2. The AI returns matched clubs and athletes, each with a clear reason for the match **and the tax benefit computed on the sponsor's own budget**.
3. The sponsor can view a matched profile and connect.

### 11.2 Why the sponsor side is the hero

The sponsor is the money. The judges in the room are themselves sponsors and brands. Showing the sponsor's view puts the judges in the buyer's seat and lets them feel the value directly: they complete a short funnel and immediately see relevant, explained opportunities they could back. That is the most persuasive possible demo for that audience.

### 11.2a The demo's two runs

The demo is built around two contrasting runs, in this order:

1. **The Estonian run (the room's run).** A local Tartu sponsor with a small budget gets Estonian matches, with the Estonian tax-free allowance surfaced on the card. This respects the fact that this is an Estonian Olympic Committee brief and shows the product solving the local problem first.
2. **The Baltic run (the money shot).** A larger Baltic-facing brand runs the same funnel and the Lithuanian club with 200% support-recipient status comes up: *"your €10,000 writes €20,000 off taxable profit."* This lands the tax differentiator and the Baltic scale story in a single screen.

The order matters. Estonia first earns the room; Lithuania second shows the ceiling.

### 11.3 What to keep as vision, not build

- The full multi-sided marketplace (shown as a vision slide).
- The verified check-in trust layer (named as the roadmap that makes matches credible).
- Baltic expansion and the cross-market product (shown as the scale story).
- Secondary revenue lines (mentioned, not built).

### 11.4 The demo narrative

Open on the problem (sport can't find sponsors, sponsors can't find sport, money stays on the sidelines — and the tax relief designed to fix that goes unclaimed). Show the sponsor completing the funnel. Show the AI returning explained matches with the after-tax number on every card. Land on the match moment twice: first a local Estonian sponsor connected to a club it would never have found, then the Baltic run where €10,000 becomes €20,000 off taxable profit. Then one slide on the model (commission on closed deals), one on scale (Baltic marketplace), and one line on the trust roadmap (verified data).

## 12. The pitch structure (for the stage)

A short stage pitch, built from this document, runs roughly:

1. **Hook.** Estonians care about sport, but only a quarter are fans and private money into sport is the smallest funding line. Meanwhile the tax relief designed to send private money into sport goes almost entirely unclaimed. Sport and sponsors cannot find each other — and when they do, nobody tells them what it actually costs.
2. **Problem.** Discovery is broken, value is unprovable, the market is fragmented and top-heavy, and the money is priced as if it were full cost when the state already agreed to subsidise it.
3. **Solution.** A marketplace that uses AI to match clubs and athletes with sponsors on audience, budget, region and goals — and prices every match after tax.
4. **Demo.** Run one: an Estonian sponsor completes the funnel and instantly sees explained matches. Run two: a Baltic brand sees €10,000 write €20,000 off taxable profit against a Lithuanian club. That second screen is the pitch.
5. **Model.** Commission on closed deals: 2% above €10,000, 10% below, plus performance. Nobody pays to join; we earn when we create value. The tax benefit we surface is worth many times the commission we charge.
6. **Scale.** Estonia first, then a Baltic-wide sponsorship marketplace no single-country or offline player can match — and the tax layer only gets stronger as it crosses borders.
7. **Trust roadmap (one line).** Verified audience data via check-in turns self-reported numbers into sponsor-grade evidence, making every match more credible over time.
8. **Close.** This is how private capital finally reaches all of Estonian and Baltic sport, not just the top.

**If asked about competition, name Sponsoo first, unprompted.** "The model is proven — Sponsoo does exactly this in Germany and it works. They are not here, and nobody anywhere prices matches after tax. That is our wedge." Volunteering the competitor and then explaining the difference is far stronger than being caught not knowing them.

Keep the money detail to one slide and the architecture to zero. The stage pitch is problem, solution, demo, model, scale. This document is the depth behind it, produced on request.

## 13. Open questions to resolve

- **Name.** The platform needs a name. Short, memorable, works across the Baltics.
- **First-market supply seeding.** Which specific Estonian clubs and athletes are the first ten profiles? Concrete names make the demo real.
- **First sponsor personas.** Which local and national brands are the demo's example sponsors? The judges' own brands (the committee's partners) are the obvious reference points.
- **Take-rate validation.** The 2% / 10% tiers and the €10,000 split are a sensible starting point but unvalidated; the pilot's job is to confirm what the market will bear.
- **Tax-rule verification.** The Lithuanian 200% deduction, the Estonian allowance thresholds, and the Latvian position each need confirming against current law with a tax adviser, including caps and the exact recipient-status requirements, before any of it is stated to a real sponsor.
- **Tax-status data source.** Where does the registry of which clubs hold qualifying recipient status come from in each country, and who keeps it current?
- **Trust-layer sequencing.** When exactly does verified check-in data get introduced, and for which clubs first?
- **Committee relationship.** Endorsement, partnership, or pilot? The nature of the committee relationship shapes how fast cold-start is solved.

## 14. Summary

Sport in Estonia and the Baltics has attention but no market to sell it. Clubs and athletes cannot be found or valued; sponsors cannot discover or evaluate them; private money stays on the sidelines — and the tax relief the Baltic states already wrote into law to pull that money in goes almost entirely unclaimed, because it never reaches the person making the decision. The solution is a two-sided marketplace with AI matching that connects sport to sponsors and prices every match after tax, monetized by commission on closed deals so nobody pays to join and the platform earns only when it creates value. The model is proven — Sponsoo runs it in Germany — but it does not exist in the Baltics, and nowhere does it carry the tax layer that makes a Lithuanian sponsorship euro worth two. The cold-start problem is solved by seeding supply, onboarding sponsors through a quiz funnel, and using the Olympic Committee as an accelerant. The credibility problem is solved over time by a verified check-in data layer that turns self-reported audience claims into sponsor-grade evidence. The scale story is a Baltic-wide sponsorship marketplace that no single-country or offline player can match. For the hackathon, the demo is the sponsor-side match flow, the most persuasive possible slice for a room full of sponsors, with the full marketplace, the trust layer, and Baltic scale shown as the vision behind it.

This directly answers the Olympic Committee's central question: how to bring more private capital into sport. By building the market that sport is missing.
